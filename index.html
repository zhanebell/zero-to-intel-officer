01FIRST PRINCIPLES

How to read Earth

Earth has no single world government. Roughly 200 states coexist in an international system. Some are wealthy, some poor; some democratic, some authoritarian; some militarily powerful, some dependent on allies. Their populations have different histories, languages, religions, identities, and interests.

Start with four questions: Who has authority? What do they want? What resources can they use? What constrains their choices? These questions apply to a village, a state, an army, or a multinational alliance.

A basic model of world politics
PeopleIdentities, needs, beliefs
→
InstitutionsGovernments, markets, armies
→
DecisionsLaw, diplomacy, trade, war
→
ConsequencesSecurity, wealth, legitimacy

Politics concerns the distribution and exercise of power. Geography shapes opportunity and vulnerability; economics allocates scarce resources; history explains inherited borders and grievances; institutions determine who can make decisions.

02THE PHYSICAL WORLD

Geography is strategic

Continents, oceans, mountain ranges, rivers, deserts, straits, and natural resources shape trade and military planning. Maritime powers rely on sea lines of communication; land powers emphasize borders and transport corridors. Geography does not dictate policy, but it changes costs and options.

Maritime chokepoints

Strait of Hormuz, Strait of Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb, Suez Canal, Panama Canal, and Turkish Straits concentrate shipping flows. Disruption can affect global energy and commerce.

Strategic regions

Indo-Pacific, Europe, Middle East, Africa, and the Americas are useful analytic frames, not perfectly bounded political units. Each contains distinct subregions and local rivalries.

Resources and infrastructure

Energy, water, food, semiconductors, critical minerals, ports, cables, and power grids create dependencies and vulnerabilities.

Demography

Population size, age structure, migration, education, and urbanization affect economic capacity, military recruitment, and political stability.

Analyst habit: never describe a country solely by its government. Examine terrain, neighbors, trade routes, population, institutions, and history.
03POLITICAL FOUNDATIONS

States, sovereignty, and legitimacy

A state is a political entity with a population, territory, governing institutions, and a claim to sovereign authority. A nation is a community of shared identity; a nation-state seeks to align the two. A government is the current leadership and institutions operating a state.

Do not confuse these layers
Population / nationPeople and identities
↓
StateTerritory, institutions, sovereign claim
↓
GovernmentCurrent decision-makers and offices
↓
PolicySpecific choices that can change without the state disappearing

Sovereignty means ultimate political authority within a territory and formal independence externally. Legitimacy means people accept an authority's right to rule. Power is the ability to influence outcomes; authority is power regarded as rightful. Borders, recognition, and sovereignty are sometimes disputed.

04POLITICAL SYSTEMS

Government systems and ideologies

Government types describe how authority is organized. Ideologies describe beliefs about what political and economic life should look like. They overlap, but are not interchangeable.

Democracy

Political authority is accountable to the public through elections and civic rights. Liberal democracies also emphasize constitutional limits, pluralism, and individual protections.

Authoritarianism

Power is concentrated and political competition is constrained. Regimes differ substantially: one-party, military, personalist, and monarchic systems.

Federal vs. unitary

Federal states divide constitutionally protected powers between national and regional governments. Unitary states concentrate ultimate constitutional authority nationally, though they may devolve powers.

Presidential vs. parliamentary

Presidential systems elect a separate executive; parliamentary executives normally depend on legislative confidence. Hybrid systems exist.

Liberalism / conservatism

Broad families of thought concerning liberty, institutions, reform, tradition, markets, and social order. Meanings vary across countries and eras.

Socialism / communism / nationalism

Socialism emphasizes social or collective economic control in varying degrees; communism has distinct theoretical and historical forms; nationalism prioritizes a national community and its political claims.

Other essential terms: capitalism, social democracy, populism, fascism, secularism, theocracy, constitutional monarchy, oligarchy, and totalitarianism. Avoid assuming a political label tells you how a state actually behaves.

05HISTORICAL FOUNDATIONS

Political history: the long arc

Prehistory–3000 BCE | Agriculture and settlements

Farming, population growth, cities, and specialized labor enable durable political hierarchies.

3000 BCE–500 CE | Early states and empires

Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, India, Persia, Greece, Rome, and other civilizations develop administration, law, armies, and trade networks.

500–1500 | Medieval and regional systems

Byzantine, Islamic, African, Asian, American, and European polities expand, trade, compete, and transmit knowledge.

1400–1800 | Oceanic expansion and colonialism

European overseas empires, conquest, slavery, commercial networks, and resistance reshape global power.

1648 | Peace of Westphalia

A landmark in European interstate diplomacy, often simplified as the birth of sovereignty; state formation was much more gradual.

1776–1800s | Revolutions and industrialization

American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions challenge imperial rule. Industrialization transforms production, transport, and warfare.

1914–1918 | World War I

Alliance systems, mobilization, industrial firepower, and imperial rivalry produce catastrophic war and political upheaval.

1917–1991 | Soviet experiment

Russian Revolution, Soviet state formation, global communist movements, and eventual Soviet dissolution.

1939–1945 | World War II

Global conflict, genocide, total war, atomic weapons, and the emergence of the United States and Soviet Union as superpowers.

1945–1991 | Cold War

Nuclear deterrence, competing alliances, proxy wars, decolonization, space competition, and ideological rivalry.

1991–2001 | Post-Cold War transition

U.S. primacy, regional conflicts, globalization, and evolving international institutions.

2001–2020 | Terrorism and counterinsurgency

9/11, Afghanistan, Iraq, counterterrorism, insurgency, and long-term questions about intervention.

2020s | Multipolar pressures and technological competition

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, U.S.–China competition, regional wars, cyber threats, supply-chain risk, and AI.

Historical narratives are contested. A serious analyst compares national perspectives, identifies whose experiences are omitted, and separates documented events from interpretations of causation.

06CONFLICT STUDIES

Why wars happen—and how they end

Wars can arise from territorial disputes, fear, misperception, alliance commitments, imperial ambitions, ideology, resource competition, domestic political incentives, or leaders' decisions. Most conflicts involve several causes.

Escalation pathway (illustrative, not inevitable)
DisputeClashing interests
→
Threat perceptionFear and uncertainty
→
MobilizationMilitary / economic signals
→
Armed conflictForce employed

Diplomacy, credible assurances, mediation, deterrence, and negotiated settlements can interrupt escalation.

Distinguish jus ad bellum (when force may lawfully be used) from jus in bello (rules governing conduct during conflict). The UN Charter generally prohibits force against another state's territorial integrity or political independence, with important exceptions such as self-defense and Security Council authorization. International humanitarian law protects civilians and limits methods of warfare.

Wars end through bargaining, military defeat, exhaustion, changes in leadership, or external pressure. Winning battles does not automatically achieve political objectives.

07AMERICAN HISTORY

How the United States emerged

Before European colonization, diverse Indigenous nations governed lands across North America. European colonization brought settlement, warfare, disease, dispossession, and forced labor. Thirteen British colonies declared independence in 1776. The Constitution was drafted in 1787 and took effect in 1789.

1776–1789

Declaration of Independence; Articles of Confederation; Constitution; Bill of Rights ratified in 1791.

1800s

Territorial expansion, Indigenous removal, slavery, sectional conflict, Civil War (1861–65), emancipation, and Reconstruction.

1898–1945

Overseas expansion, two world wars, Great Depression, New Deal, and emergence as a global military and economic power.

1945–1991

Cold War, Korean and Vietnam wars, civil rights movement, nuclear strategy, and major domestic social changes.

1991–present

Globalization, 9/11, wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, financial crisis, political polarization, technological change, and renewed great-power competition.

The U.S. is a constitutional federal republic with representative democracy. Its founding principles coexist with historical failures and ongoing debates over rights, representation, federal power, and national identity.

08U.S. GOVERNMENT

The Constitution and three branches

The Constitution is the supreme law of the United States. It creates a federal government with enumerated powers and separates power among legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Checks and balances make each branch dependent on, and capable of constraining, others.

Federal separation of powers
U.S. ConstitutionSupreme legal framework
↓
CongressArticle I · House + Senate
Legislation, appropriations, oversight, declare war
PresidentArticle II · Executive branch
Execute laws, diplomacy, commander in chief
Federal courtsArticle III · Supreme Court + lower courts
Resolve cases and interpret law

The House is apportioned by population, with two-year terms. The Senate has two members per state, with six-year staggered terms. The president serves a four-year term, selected through the Electoral College. Federal judges generally hold office during good behavior.

Federalism: states retain substantial powers over education, policing, elections administration, and other areas, subject to constitutional and federal-law limits. Local governments derive authority primarily from states.

Critical distinction: the president commands the armed forces, but Congress funds them, regulates them, and has the constitutional power to declare war. Modern uses of force also involve statutory authorizations and contested war-powers practice.
09POLICY PROCESS

How U.S. national security decisions happen

National security policy combines presidential direction, congressional law and funding, agency expertise, diplomatic negotiation, intelligence assessments, military advice, and operational implementation.

Simplified decision and execution model
President / National Security Council processObjectives, options, risk decisions
↑ advice / ↓ direction
State DepartmentDiplomacy and foreign policy
Defense DepartmentMilitary planning and force
Intelligence CommunityAssessments and warning
↕ Congressional legislation, funding, and oversight
Implementation and feedbackDiplomatic actions, operations, assessments, revision

The National Security Council is a presidential advisory and coordination system, not a fourth branch. The State Department leads much diplomacy; Treasury can apply financial sanctions; Homeland Security addresses domestic security missions; the Intelligence Community provides information and assessments. Interagency coordination is essential because instruments of national power overlap.

A common mnemonic is DIME: diplomatic, informational, military, and economic instruments. It is a framework, not a complete account of all tools of statecraft.

10WORLD ORDER

International institutions and alliances

The United Nations provides a forum for diplomacy, humanitarian coordination, international law, and collective security. Its Security Council has five permanent members—China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States—with veto power over substantive resolutions, plus ten elected members.

United Nations

General Assembly, Security Council, International Court of Justice, Secretariat, and specialized agencies. It is not a world government.

NATO

Collective-defense alliance centered on Europe and North America. Article 5 treats an armed attack against one ally as an attack against all, with members determining their responses.

European Union

Supranational political and economic union with shared institutions, a single market, and, for many members, a common currency.

Other major frameworks

ASEAN, African Union, G7, G20, WTO, IMF, World Bank, BRICS, and regional security arrangements. Their memberships, powers, and goals differ.

Allies have formal or enduring security commitments; partners cooperate without necessarily having the same obligations. Coalitions are often mission-specific. International law, diplomatic recognition, and treaty obligations affect what states can lawfully and practically do.

11MILITARY HISTORY

How warfare evolved

Ancient / classical warfare

Infantry formations, cavalry, fortifications, logistics, and siegecraft; campaigns constrained by food and movement.

Gunpowder era

Firearms, artillery, professional armies, naval gunnery, and increasingly centralized fiscal-military states.

Industrial warfare

Railways, telegraph, mass production, machine guns, and industrial logistics increase scale and lethality.

World War II

Combined arms, mechanization, strategic airpower, amphibious operations, radar, codebreaking, and atomic weapons.

Cold War

Nuclear deterrence, ballistic missiles, intelligence satellites, electronic warfare, and proxy conflicts.

Contemporary warfare

Precision strike, unmanned systems, space-enabled operations, cyber capabilities, information warfare, and contested logistics.

Technology changes tactics but does not erase the enduring importance of command, morale, terrain, logistics, intelligence, political purpose, and adaptation. Study campaigns by asking what each side believed, what it knew, what it could sustain, and how its decisions changed outcomes.

12DEFENSE ENTERPRISE

How the U.S. Department of Defense works

The Department of Defense (DoD) is a cabinet-level executive department responsible for military forces and defense policy. The Secretary of Defense is the principal civilian leader under the president. The Joint Chiefs of Staff provide military advice; combatant commanders exercise operational command authority through the chain of command.

Operational chain of command (simplified)
PresidentCommander in Chief
↓
Secretary of DefenseCivilian leadership and direction
↓
Combatant CommandersGeographic and functional joint commands
↓
Assigned operational forcesJoint / component / subordinate commands
Organize, train, equip vs. employ
Military DepartmentsArmy · Navy/Marine Corps · Air Force/Space Force
Generate and sustain forces
Joint Chiefs of StaffSenior military advice and joint coordination
Not an operational command chain
Combatant CommandsPlan and execute assigned joint missions
Employ assigned forces

The six armed services are Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Air Force, Space Force, and Coast Guard. The Coast Guard normally operates under Homeland Security, but may serve in the Navy when directed under law. The National Guard has state and federal roles; reserve components support mobilization and sustained operations.

Combatant commands: geographic commands include INDOPACOM, EUCOM, CENTCOM, AFRICOM, NORTHCOM, and SOUTHCOM. Functional commands include STRATCOM, SOCOM, TRANSCOM, CYBERCOM, and SPACECOM. Verify current command organization through official sources.

Terminology note (2026): “Department of War” has been used as a secondary executive-branch designation; the statutory department established under Title 10 remains the Department of Defense unless changed by law. Always distinguish official naming practice from legal structure.
13MILITARY ORGANIZATION

Ranks, command, and joint operations

Enlisted personnel provide technical expertise and execute missions; noncommissioned officers lead and develop personnel; commissioned officers hold leadership and command responsibilities. Warrant officers serve specialized technical and leadership roles in services that use them.

O-4 / O-5 / O-6Major / Lt Col / Colonel

Rank is not identical to command authority. Command is a legally defined responsibility; staff officers advise and coordinate. A joint force includes more than one service; combined or multinational operations include forces from multiple nations. Tactical actions support operational campaigns, which should serve strategic political objectives.

Strategic level

National goals, alliances, deterrence, resource allocation, and war termination.

Operational level

Campaign design, theater objectives, sequencing, sustainment, and joint integration.

Tactical level

Engagements, missions, units, and immediate military tasks.

Enablers

Logistics, communications, medical support, intelligence, engineering, and force protection.

14STRATEGIC STUDIES

Strategy, deterrence, and nuclear forces

Strategy connects ends (objectives), ways (methods), and means (resources), while managing risk. A plan can be tactically effective yet strategically counterproductive if it fails to produce a sustainable political result.

Ends–ways–means model
ENDSWhat outcome?
↔
WAYSHow to achieve it?
↔
MEANSWith what capabilities?
↔
RISKWhat can fail?

Deterrence aims to prevent an action by shaping an adversary's expected costs or prospects of success. Compellence aims to change behavior already underway. Nuclear deterrence rests partly on credible retaliatory capability, survivability, communication, and perceptions. Miscalculation remains possible.

The nuclear triad refers to land-based intercontinental ballistic missiles, submarine-launched ballistic missiles, and strategic bombers. Arms control, nonproliferation, extended deterrence, and escalation management are central to strategic stability.

15INTELLIGENCE FOUNDATIONS

The U.S. Intelligence Community

Intelligence is information and analysis developed to reduce uncertainty for decision-makers. It does not eliminate uncertainty, and it should not be shaped to support a preferred policy outcome. The U.S. Intelligence Community (IC) consists of 18 elements, coordinated by the Director of National Intelligence.

Selected intelligence organizations and roles
Director of National Intelligence (DNI)Community integration and national intelligence leadership
↓ coordination / oversight relationships vary
CIAForeign intelligence, all-source analysis, authorized covert action
NSASignals intelligence and cybersecurity missions
DIADefense intelligence and military assessments
NGAGeospatial intelligence
NRONational reconnaissance systems
Service intelligenceArmy, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, Space Force

Other elements include FBI intelligence, State Department INR, Treasury OIA, DHS I&A, Coast Guard Intelligence, DEA intelligence, DOE intelligence/counterintelligence, and others. Their missions, collection authorities, and oversight differ. The DNI is not the operational commander of every agency.

Intelligence work is constrained by U.S. law, executive orders, classification rules, civil liberties protections, and congressional and executive oversight. Collection authorities differ for foreign intelligence and domestic activities.

16ANALYTIC METHODS

Intelligence collection and analytic tradecraft

The intelligence cycle is a useful conceptual model, though real work is iterative. Requirements drive collection; information is processed, evaluated, analyzed, disseminated, and revised as customers ask new questions.

The intelligence cycle
DirectionDefine intelligence need
→
CollectionGather relevant information
→
ProcessingMake data usable
→
AnalysisAssess meaning and uncertainty
→
DisseminationInform decision-makers

Feedback and new requirements return the process to earlier stages.

HUMINT

Human-source intelligence: information derived from people and human interactions.

SIGINT

Signals intelligence: intelligence derived from signals, subject to applicable authorities.

GEOINT / IMINT

Geospatial analysis and imagery-based information about activities, locations, and physical features.

MASINT

Measurement and signature intelligence derived from distinctive physical phenomena.

OSINT

Open-source intelligence from publicly or commercially available information, evaluated systematically.

All-source analysis

Integrates multiple streams to evaluate hypotheses, explain patterns, and communicate judgments.

How an analyst thinks

Separate observation from inference; assess source reliability and information credibility; consider alternative hypotheses; identify assumptions and indicators; communicate confidence and uncertainty. High confidence does not mean certainty. Avoid mirror-imaging, confirmation bias, and mistaking a plausible narrative for a proven explanation.

Analytic judgment structure
QuestionWhat decision needs support?
↓
Evidence and source evaluationWhat do we know? What is missing?
↓
Competing explanationsWhat else could account for the evidence?
↓
AssessmentJudgment + confidence + implications + indicators
17OFFICER APPLICATION

From student to Air Force intelligence officer

Air Force intelligence officers (14N) help commanders understand adversaries, operational environments, capabilities, intentions, and risks. Depending on assignment, they may support air operations, targeting processes, mission planning, intelligence preparation of the operational environment, indications and warning, or staff-level assessments.

Intelligence supports command decisions
Commander’s decisionWhat must be decided?
←
Intel assessmentAdversary, terrain, threats, uncertainty
←
Sources and analystsCollection, evaluation, fusion

A junior officer must do more than memorize organizations. They must lead teams, communicate concisely, manage classified information correctly, recognize the limits of intelligence, and deliver timely assessments without overstating confidence. A good briefing clearly separates facts, judgments, assumptions, and recommended follow-up collection.

Professional foundations

Ethics, lawful orders, civil-military relations, writing, briefing, team leadership, and security discipline.

Technical foundations

Geography, order of battle, military capabilities, data literacy, intelligence disciplines, operational planning, and cyber/space awareness.

Strategic foundations

Adversary doctrine, alliances, deterrence, regional history, international law, and national security policy.

Analytic foundations

Structured reasoning, uncertainty, hypothesis testing, sourcing, collection gaps, and decision relevance.

This is an unclassified educational overview—not an official 14N qualification course, operational doctrine, or a substitute for service training.
18CONTEMPORARY STRATEGY · NOVEMBER 2026

Reading the world today

Current geopolitics changes faster than a static textbook. This chapter describes enduring issues relevant in the mid-2020s, not a verified live operational or diplomatic update for 10 November 2026. Use the official sources below for the latest status before briefing anyone.

INDO-PACIFIC

China, Taiwan, and maritime competition

Analyze China's economic and military capabilities, Taiwan Strait stability, South China Sea disputes, U.S. alliances, technology controls, and crisis escalation risks. Distinguish Beijing's claims, Taipei's positions, and Washington's policies.

EUROPE

Russia, Ukraine, and NATO

Study Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine beginning in 2022, European security, military assistance, sanctions, nuclear signaling, and the implications for NATO deterrence. Verify battlefield and diplomatic developments separately.

MIDDLE EAST

Regional conflict and security

Understand Israel–Palestinian history, Gaza, Iran, Gulf states, proxy networks, maritime security, energy markets, and the humanitarian dimensions of conflict. Avoid treating the region as a single political actor.

KOREAN PENINSULA

North Korea and deterrence

Examine nuclear and missile programs, the Korean War armistice, the U.S.–South Korea alliance, China's role, and escalation risks.

GLOBAL SOUTH

Agency beyond great powers

Examine African, Latin American, South Asian, and Southeast Asian states as actors with their own priorities—not merely as arenas of U.S.–China competition.

CROSS-DOMAIN

Cyber, space, AI, and economic security

Semiconductors, critical infrastructure, satellite dependence, influence operations, cyber intrusion, supply chains, and emerging technology shape both military and civilian resilience.

What to monitor in any crisis

Track the actors, stated objectives, force posture, diplomatic channels, economic dependencies, domestic political incentives, civilian effects, escalation thresholds, and observable indicators. Distinguish confirmed facts from rumors, and date every time-sensitive assessment.
